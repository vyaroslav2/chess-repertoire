### B1 — Build and score the Black human-move shortlist

**What the code does**  
B1 receives a position where Black is to move from [[A4]].

It first calls [[F]] to get the human move data for that position:

- Masters;
    
- Elite;
    
- Amateur.
    

B1 then builds one combined entry per move using **Masters and Elite only**. Amateur data is fetched because [[F]] always returns all three datasets, but B1 does not use Amateur when scoring Black's candidate replies.

It starts with the Masters list.

For each Masters move, it records:

- SAN;
    
- number of Masters games;
    
- Masters Black wins;
    
- Masters draws;
    
- Masters White wins.
    

It initially gives that move zero Elite counts.

B1 then walks through the Elite list.

If an Elite move already exists in the Masters-derived map, B1 adds its Elite statistics to that existing entry.

If the move was not present in Masters, B1 creates a new entry with:

- zero Masters counts;
    
- the Elite counts for that move.
    

So the candidate pool is the **union of the Masters and Elite move lists**.

A move does not need to occur in both datasets to become a candidate. A move seen only in Elite can still enter the pool. A move seen only in Amateur cannot.

For every combined move, B1 calculates a weighted game count:

`weighted games = Masters games × 5 + Elite games`

It applies the same weighting to Black wins:

`weighted Black wins = Masters Black wins × 5 + Elite Black wins`

and to draws:

`weighted draws = Masters draws × 5 + Elite draws`

So one Masters game is deliberately treated as having five times the weight of one Elite game.

B1 then calculates the move's Black score:

`score = (weighted Black wins + 0.5 × weighted draws + 24) / (weighted games + 50)`

The `24` and `50` form a prior:

- imagine 50 extra games;
    
- imagine Black scored 24 points from them;
    
- prior score = `24 / 50 = 48%`.
    

This pulls small samples towards a neutral-ish 48% Black score rather than allowing a handful of games to produce extreme rankings.

For example, suppose a move has:

- 10 Masters games;
    
- 20 Elite games;
    
- 4 Masters Black wins;
    
- 8 Elite Black wins;
    
- 3 Masters draws;
    
- 6 Elite draws.
    

Then:

`weighted games = 10 × 5 + 20 = 70`

`weighted Black wins = 4 × 5 + 8 = 28`

`weighted draws = 3 × 5 + 6 = 21`

and:

`score = (28 + 0.5 × 21 + 24) / (70 + 50)`

`= 62.5 / 120`

`≈ 52.1%`

So this score is a **smoothed Black result score**, not move popularity and not an engine evaluation.

B1 then removes every move with fewer than:

`15 weighted games`

A move with only 2 Masters games would therefore have:

`2 × 5 = 10`

and be dropped if it had no Elite games.

A move with 3 Masters games would have:

`3 × 5 = 15`

and would meet the threshold even with no Elite games.

The surviving moves are sorted from highest score to lowest score.

That ordered list becomes the candidate shortlist passed to [[B2 draft]] for engine evaluation.

The shortlist is allowed to be empty.

**Why this matters**  
B1 decides which human Black replies deserve engine verification and in what order they should be considered.

It does not simply choose:

> "the most popular Black move"

and it does not choose:

> "the engine's best move".

Instead, it asks roughly:

> "Among moves actually played by strong humans, which moves have produced the best smoothed results for Black?"

Masters games are deliberately given extra influence.

The engine stages that follow can then reject human moves that are not objectively acceptable.

This means the intended structure is:

```text
strong-human data
        ↓
rank promising human replies
        ↓
engine verification
        ↓
choose the first acceptable reply
```

The human statistics therefore decide the **preference order**, while later engine logic decides whether a move is safe enough to accept.

**Why it may have been designed this way**  
Known: the aim is to produce repertoire replies that are both:

- supported by strong human practice;
    
- objectively acceptable according to the engine.
    

The five-times Masters weighting gives over-the-board master practice more influence than the 2500 Lichess group without discarding the larger Elite dataset.

The 48%-from-50-games prior prevents tiny samples from dominating the ordering.

The 15-weighted-game floor adds another protection against extremely sparse evidence.
